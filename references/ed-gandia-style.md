# Ed Gandia Storytelling Style — Ghost-Writer

Ed Gandia is the master of warm, conversational B2B copy — the "smart friend explaining something" voice. His writing feels like a conversation, not a pitch. Apply this to every draft.

## The Core Rules

### 1. Write like you talk

If you wouldn't say it out loud to a friend, don't write it. Read the draft aloud. If it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.

**Bad:** "Our comprehensive suite of solutions empowers businesses to achieve their growth objectives."
**Good:** "Here's the thing most people get wrong about Google Ads."

### 2. Short sentences. Short paragraphs.

Gandia's signature is rhythm. Short punch. Medium flow. Long build. Fragment. The reader should feel the cadence, not fight through walls of text.

**Rule:** 2-4 sentence paragraphs. One idea per paragraph. Vary sentence length.

### 3. Tell the story before the lesson

Don't lead with the principle. Lead with the moment. The story makes the lesson land.

**Bad:** "Conversion rate optimization is important because..."
**Good:** "A client called me last Tuesday. He'd spent $4,000 on ads and gotten two leads. Both were wrong numbers."

### 4. Make the reader feel understood first

Before you make a point, show the reader you get their situation. Empathy before argument.

**Bad:** "You need to optimize your landing page."
**Good:** "I know what it's like to stare at a dashboard full of numbers and not know which one matters. That's where most business owners are."

### 5. Use "you" and "I" like a conversation

Gandia writes like he's sitting across from you. Direct address. First person. No corporate "we."

**Bad:** "Businesses should consider..."
**Good:** "Here's what I'd do if I were you."

---

## The Gandia Voice Checklist

- [ ] Does it sound like a person talking, or a company writing?
- [ ] Are the sentences short enough to read aloud without running out of breath?
- [ ] Is there a story or moment, not just a principle?
- [ ] Does the reader feel understood before being instructed?
- [ ] Is it warm, or is it cold and clinical?
- [ ] Would the reader nod along, or skim past?

---

## The Gandia Opening Formula

1. **Acknowledge the reader's situation** — "I know what it's like to..."
2. **Name the specific pain** — "You're spending money and not seeing it come back."
3. **Drop a story or moment** — "A client of mine was in the exact same spot."
4. **Bridge to the lesson** — "Here's what changed for him."

---

## The Gandia Close Formula

1. **Restate the transformation** — "That's the difference between paying for clicks and paying for customers."
2. **Make it personal** — "You don't need another agency. You need to understand your own numbers."
3. **End on a warm, low-pressure note** — "Let's sit down and figure it out together."

---

## Gandia Anti-Patterns (Never Use)

- Corporate jargon ("synergy," "leverage," "optimize" as a buzzword)
- Cold, clinical tone ("It is recommended that...")
- Walls of text with no rhythm
- Leading with credentials instead of empathy
- "We" language when "you" and "I" would be warmer
- Any sentence that sounds like it came from a press release
